# Benchmarking the Generalization and Extrapolation Limits of Composition-Based Machine Learning Models for Perovskite Formation Energy and Band-Gap Prediction

**Authors:** Muhammad Kashan Ali

**Affiliation:** Department of Chemistry, Bahauddin Zakariya University, Multan, Pakistan

**Abstract**

Machine-learning models for perovskites are usually tested on random splits, where the test crystals resemble the training crystals. That test measures interpolation, not the transfer that materials discovery actually needs: predicting a crystal that contains an element the model has never seen. We benchmark that transfer on a single clean dataset — the Castelli set of 18,928 ABX$_3$ perovskites, a complete 52 A × 52 B × 7 X factorial grid — using composition-only (Magpie) features and no density-functional-theory (DFT) derived inputs. On random and composition-grouped cross-validation, tuned models reach R² 0.431 and 0.443 (XGBoost) for formation energy per atom, below a pooled composition-only ceiling of R² 0.543 that follows from A/B-swapped crystal pairs sharing identical Magpie features but different energies. On leave-one-B-out extrapolation the same models collapse (pooled R² 0.206 for ridge regression, 0.086 for XGBoost), and ridge regression fails catastrophically on leave-one-X-out (pooled R² −9.958). Band-gap prediction is dominated by a 96.1% zero class: a classifier ranks metals against non-metals well (ROC-AUC 0.919) but finds only 8.0% of non-metals at the standard 0.5 threshold, and regression on the 735 non-zero gaps reaches R² 0.558 under grouped validation. Split-conformal intervals at a nominal 90% level cover 0.899 of random test rows but range from 0.632 (rhenium) to 0.984 (zinc) across nine held-out B metals under family shift — coverage under shift is metal-dependent, not guaranteed, and fails exactly where point predictions fail. Site-aware features that give each lattice site its own elemental properties recover leave-one-B-out performance to pooled R² 0.733 for XGBoost, a result verified against leakage with four diagnostics. An ablation that removes all B-site information still reaches 0.683, showing that most of the gain comes from resolving the A and X sites explicitly, with B-site physics adding only +0.050. SHAP analysis shows the site-aware model leans on Magpie statistics plus B-site physics on familiar crystals, and still leans on Magpie for unseen metals on average (42% of the credit) — except tungsten, where B-site physics leads and is also necessary. A pretrained graph network (MEGNet), evaluated zero-shot on the same split, reaches pooled R² 0.561 after recalibration (Spearman 0.752): it beats composition-only by a wide margin but does not beat the site-aware model. The message is threefold: composition-only models interpolate but do not extrapolate to new B-site metals; site resolution — not more composition statistics — is what restores transfer; and structure alone, as embodied in a pretrained graph network, does not close the gap that site resolution closes.

## 1. Introduction

Perovskites of the form ABX$_3$ are among the most studied materials in photovoltaics, in large part because their chemistry is combinatorial: changing the A-site cation, the B-site metal, or the X-site anion tunes the band gap and the stability almost independently, with the B and X sites setting the band edges and the A site controlling size and structural stability. Machine learning has been applied to this space for a decade, and reported accuracies are high: gradient-boosted models reach R² 0.873 for band gaps on a few thousand perovskites [6], and R² 0.928 for formation energy [7]. Graph neural networks that read the crystal structure directly do better still, and structure-aware models dominate the MatBench perovskite leaderboard [3,10]. These numbers raise an obvious question that they do not answer: what happens when the model is asked about an element it has never seen? A screening campaign that only ever proposes elements already in the training set is not discovering anything, and a model that is only tested on random splits has never been asked the question that discovery poses.

This paper benchmarks exactly that question. We use one dataset, the Castelli set of 18,928 perovskites [1], in full, and we test four things in sequence: (A) composition-level prediction on familiar crystals, (B) extrapolation to unseen B-site metals and X-site anions, (C) band-gap screening under the severe class imbalance of the data, and (D) whether conformal uncertainty intervals keep their promise under the same shift. We then test one constructive step — site-aware features — on the hardest split only, explain what the model relies on with SHAP, and compare against a pretrained graph network (MEGNet) on the same split. The design is deliberately austere: composition and structure only, no DFT-derived quantities as inputs, pooled (not per-family mean) reporting for every extrapolation claim, and every number traceable to a saved results file. The contribution is not a new model and not a new material. It is a measured answer to the transfer question, including the limits, the failure modes, and the one intervention that demonstrably helps. A literature check (§5.3) places the contribution precisely: two of its five claims are new and three are partial, with the priors cited.

## 2. Data

The dataset is the `castelli_perovskites` set distributed with matminer [12], containing 18,928 perovskites generated by ABX combinatorics and computed by Castelli *et al.* [1]. We use it alone. The MatBench perovskite task [3] is the same 18,928 rows in the same order with identical formation energies, so merging it would add nothing; merging on reduced formula is in any case wrong, because it collapses A/B-swapped crystals that share a reduced formula but have different structures and different energies.

Each structure has five sites (one A, one B, three X), so the target is formation energy per atom, e$_{\rm form}$/5. The target definition was verified against the source documentation before use: the MatBench entry states that e$_{\rm form}$ is the "heat of formation of the entire 5-atom perovskite cell, in eV as calculated by RPBE GGA-DFT", with the oxygen reference state taken from the chemical potential of oxygen in water vapour rather than from O$_2$ molecules [3]. The dataset is a complete factorial grid: 52 A-site elements × 52 B-site elements × 7 X-site anion types (FNO, FO$_2$, N$_2$O, N$_3$, NO$_2$, O$_2$S, O$_3$), giving 18,928 rows with exactly 364 rows per B-site element and 2,704 rows per anion type. A, B, and X were assigned from structure site order only (site 0 = A, site 1 = B, sites 2–4 = X), validated on all rows: 0 unparseable, 0 true mismatches. An earlier radius-based assignment rule was tested against site order, failed validation, and was discarded.

The target distribution is narrow and mostly positive: e$_{\rm form}$ per atom has mean 0.294, standard deviation 0.149, minimum −0.128, median 0.272, and maximum 1.032 eV/atom, with only 74 of 18,928 rows negative. Because no energy-above-hull data exist for this set, no claim about thermodynamic stability is made anywhere in this paper; formation energy alone is not stability. The band gap is the DFT (GLLBSC) gap, not an experimental gap, and every band-gap claim in this paper is a claim about predicting the DFT target. The gap is zero for 18,193 of 18,928 rows (96.1%); only 735 rows have a non-zero gap (mean 1.824, median 1.4, maximum 7.0 eV), and only 77 rows fall in the 1.3–1.6 eV range usually quoted as ideal for single-junction solar cells.

The factorial structure creates the central difficulty of the dataset. There are 18,928 unique ordered formulas but only 9,646 unique reduced formulas, meaning 9,282 reduced formulas appear twice (once as ABX$_3$ and once as BAX$_3$). Crystals that share a reduced formula — most importantly A/B-swapped pairs — have identical composition and therefore identical Magpie features, but different formation energies. A composition-only model cannot distinguish them, which imposes a pooled ceiling of R² 0.543 on any Magpie-only model: that is the R² of a model that knew each composition's true group mean. This ceiling is a property of the data and the feature set, not of any particular algorithm, and it is the benchmark against which Study A must be read.

## 3. Methods

**Features.** The baseline feature set is Magpie [2] as implemented in matminer (`ElementProperty.from_preset('magpie')`): 132 columns, 0 missing values. Magpie is composition-only by construction, which is the point of Studies A–D. No DFT-derived column is ever used as a feature: valence-band maximum, conduction-band minimum, Fermi level, Fermi width, magnetic moment, formation energy, and band gap are all excluded by an explicit leakage assertion, checked before any model is fitted. The valence- and conduction-band edges define the gap directly (gap = CBM − VBM), so including them would be using the answer.

**Site-aware features (Stage 3b).** The site-aware set has 232 columns: Magpie (132) plus a one-hot encoding of the B site (52) and the X anion (7), per-site elemental physics (36: 12 properties each for A, B, and X — atomic number, atomic mass, electronegativity, ionic radius, atomic radius, first ionisation energy, electron affinity, valence s/p/d/f electron counts, and Mendeleev number), and five derived quantities (tolerance factor t = (r$_A$ + r$_X$)/(√2(r$_B$ + r$_X$)), octahedral factor μ = r$_B$/r$_X$, r$_A$ − r$_B$, and the A–B and B–X electronegativity differences). Ionic radii use `Element.ionic_radii` with a documented charge convention (A: smallest positive charge; B: largest positive charge; X: most negative charge), giving 0 missing values. No A-site one-hot is included. The B-site one-hot cannot transfer to a new B metal — for a held-out metal it is all zero in training — and the experiment is designed so that transfer, if it occurs, must come from the physics features.

**No-B-information ablation (Stage 3c).** To separate the effect of site resolution from the effect of B-site physics, a third feature set drops all B information: the 52 B one-hot columns, the 12 B-site physics columns, and all five derived columns, leaving 163 columns (Magpie 132 + A-site physics 12 + X-site physics 12 + X one-hot 7). The drop is asserted in code. This variant can still work in principle, because Magpie still carries the B contribution in aggregate form, and with A and X given explicitly the model can isolate the B contribution by subtraction.

**Feature attribution (Stages 9a and 9a2).** SHAP (SHapley Additive exPlanations) values were computed with `shap.TreeExplainer` for the tuned XGBoost models. Stage 9a trains site-aware and Magpie-only models on a random 80/20 split (seed 0) and computes SHAP on a 2,000-row test subsample; Stage 9a2 trains the site-aware model on rows with B ≠ the held-out metal for each of Te, W, and Mg and computes SHAP on all 364 held-out rows, reporting the held-out R² alongside the shares. Features are grouped as Magpie (132), A-physics (12), B-physics (12), X-physics (12), derived (5), B one-hot (52), and X one-hot (7). The SHAP identity (base value + Σ SHAP = prediction) was asserted within 10⁻⁶ on five rows per model and family (maximum errors 4.7×10⁻⁸ and 1.5×10⁻⁷ in Stage 9a; 1.4×10⁻⁷, 6.2×10⁻⁸, and 8.8×10⁻⁸ for Te, W, and Mg in Stage 9a2). SHAP measures reliance — what the fitted model leans on — not necessity; necessity is measured by the ablations in §4.5, and the two must not be confused (see §4.6).

**Graph-model comparison (Stage 9b).** The pretrained MEGNet formation-energy model Eform_MP_2019 [5], trained by its authors on 133,420 Materials Project structures (April 2019 snapshot; the model's own reported test MAE is 0.026 eV/atom on Materials Project data), was evaluated zero-shot on the same leave-one-B-out split: it was never trained on our data. Predictions use the model exactly as shipped (megnet 1.3.2, TensorFlow 2.21.0), with two compatibility workarounds documented in the stage file (a pymatgen class-alias shim and loading with compile=False, since prediction needs no optimizer). Because MEGNet's energies are on the Materials Project scale and ours are on the Castelli scale (oxygen referenced to water vapour, not O₂), two honest metrics are reported: Spearman rank correlation, which is scale-free, and a calibrated R² from a two-parameter affine rescaling (y_Castelli = a·y_MEGNet + b) fitted per family on a 2,000-row random pool (seed 0) excluding that family's rows (n ≈ 1,950–1,977 per fit). The raw R² is reported only to show the scale offset, never as a performance claim. Contamination was measured against the October 2018 Materials Project snapshot (83,989 rows): 1,175 of 18,928 Castelli rows (6.2%) share a reduced formula with it, concentrated in the oxides, so the zero-shot numbers are an upper bound for a clean pretrained model. Because the model was trained on the April 2019 snapshot (133,420 structures) while the overlap was measured against the October 2018 snapshot, 6.2% is a lower bound on the true overlap. Training a graph model from scratch on this grid was not completed here and remains future work.

**Models.** Four models are used in Study A: a mean baseline, ridge regression (with standardisation, α = 1.0), a random forest (100 trees, maximum depth 10), and tuned XGBoost (maximum depth 3, 100 trees, learning rate 0.05, subsample 0.8, column subsample 0.8, L2 penalty 5). Studies B–D and the site-aware experiments use ridge regression and tuned XGBoost only. Default tree settings are never used: in the first version of this work, default XGBoost and random forest scored below the mean baseline on this data. The tuned configurations were fixed from a diagnostic fold in the first version of the work and were not re-tuned on the version-2 test data; they are therefore slightly optimistic, and we disclose that here rather than claiming untouched test scores.

**Splits and metrics.** Study A uses random 5-fold and grouped-by-composition (reduced formula) 5-fold cross-validation, seeds 0, 1, and 2; grouped folds use GroupKFold without shuffling, so grouped results are identical across seeds, as expected. Study B uses leave-one-B-out (52 families) and leave-one-X-out (7 families). The headline extrapolation metric is always the **pooled** R², RMSE, and MAE computed over all held-out predictions together; per-family mean, median, minimum, and maximum R² are reported as secondary context only, because per-family R² is misleading when within-family variance is small. Study C uses grouped 5-fold splits only, so that no composition appears in both training and test. Study D uses split conformal prediction at a nominal 90% level with a 60/20/20 train/calibration/test split, seed 0, and the exact order-statistic quantile: calibration residuals r = |y − ŷ| are sorted, k = ⌈(n$_{\rm cal}$ + 1) × 0.90⌉, and q is the k-th smallest residual (or the maximum residual if k exceeds n$_{\rm cal}$); intervals are ŷ ± q and the mean width is 2q. Coverage is always reported together with mean width and point R², because coverage alone is not accuracy.

**Leakage checks.** Every stage asserts that no forbidden column is in the feature matrix, verifies row-by-row alignment between the feature frame and the audited data, and (for family-out conformal tests) asserts that the held-out metal appears zero times in training and calibration. One pre-registered safeguard deserves an explicit note: before the site-aware experiment was run, a leak alarm was set at pooled leave-one-B-out R² above 0.60, on the prior belief that such a score must indicate leakage. The alarm fired (0.733), and the result was therefore investigated with four diagnostics before being reported: the baseline was reproduced exactly; dropping the B one-hot barely moved the score, as expected for a column that is all zero in training for a held-out metal; leave-one-element-out (removing the held-out element as an A site as well as a B site, which also removes every A/B-swapped twin of the test rows) held the score; and feeding deliberately wrong B-site physics values to the test rows destroyed the score. No leak was found. The 0.60 alarm was set before seeing data and was too low; it has been retired, and the 0.733 result is reported with the diagnostics, not in spite of them.

**Software and reproducibility.** All version-2 results were produced in Google Colab with scikit-learn 1.6.1, XGBoost 3.4.1, matminer 0.10.1, and pymatgen 2026.10.24. MEGNet 1.3.2 with TensorFlow 2.21.0 was used for Stage 9b; SHAP was used for Stages 9a and 9a2. Every results file has one fixed column set, every result row is written to disk as soon as it finishes, and every stage cell is safe to re-run: finished folds and families are skipped, not repeated. All figures are generated from the saved results files, never typed by hand.

## 4. Results

### 4.1 Study A — Composition-level prediction (interpolation)

On random 5-fold cross-validation (seeds 0–2), Magpie-only models give mean R² 0.385 (ridge), 0.259 (random forest), and 0.431 (tuned XGBoost); the mean baseline scores 0 by construction. On grouped-by-composition folds the corresponding means are 0.388, 0.475, and 0.443. The best result, grouped random forest at 0.475, sits below the pooled composition-only ceiling of 0.543, as it must: the ceiling is the score of a model that knew each composition's true group mean, and no composition-only model can exceed it. The narrow gap between random and grouped splits is itself informative — grouped splits are usually harder, but here removing a test row's compositional twin from training removes a source of confusion rather than a source of help, so grouped performance does not fall. Figure 1 (`fig1_cv_r2.png`) shows the comparison with the ceiling line. The conclusion of Study A is modest and it is the baseline for everything that follows: Magpie-only models interpolate this grid to roughly R² 0.43–0.48, and they cannot do better without features that distinguish crystals of the same composition.

### 4.2 Study B — Extrapolation to unseen B metals and X anions

Leave-one-B-out is the paper's main test, and Magpie-only models fail it. Pooled over all 18,928 held-out predictions, ridge regression reaches R² 0.206 (RMSE 0.132, MAE 0.102 eV/atom) and tuned XGBoost reaches R² 0.086 (RMSE 0.142, MAE 0.107 eV/atom) — down from 0.43 on random splits. Per-family R² is worse still (ridge mean −0.774, median −0.469, worst W −5.103, best Mg 0.308; XGBoost mean −0.941, median −0.607, worst Re −4.642, best Mg 0.431), but the pooled number is the headline: averaged over all held-out crystals, the model explains almost none of the variance of a new B metal's formation energies. Leave-one-X-out separates the two model classes. Ridge regression extrapolates linearly in composition space and fails catastrophically (pooled R² −9.958, RMSE 0.492, MAE 0.148 eV/atom; worst family FNO −84.581; per-family mean −12.520, median −0.169, best N$_2$O 0.377). XGBoost, which cannot extrapolate beyond its training range and instead assigns unseen anions to the nearest leaf, survives (pooled R² 0.375, RMSE 0.117, MAE 0.090 eV/atom; per-family mean 0.283, median 0.246, minimum FO$_2$ 0.182, maximum N$_2$O 0.476, all seven families positive). Figure 2 (`fig2_familyout_r2.png`) shows the pooled comparison; the X-out ridge bar is drawn truncated and annotated as off scale, because plotting −9.958 on the same axis as the other values would crush the chart. Figure 3 (`fig3_parity_xgb.png`) shows the B-out parity plots. The conclusion is sharp: a composition-only model is an interpolation model. It does not generalise to a new B-site metal, and a linear model does not generalise to a new anion at all.

### 4.3 Study C — Band-gap screening

The band gap cannot be treated as an ordinary regression target: 96.1% of the values are zero (Figure 4, `fig4_distributions.png`). Plain all-rows regression gives R² 0.288, a number that looks respectable only because the model predicts zero almost everywhere; it is reported here as a warning only, never as a result. The analysis is therefore split in two. First, classification of metal (gap = 0) against non-metal (gap > 0) with grouped splits: tuned XGBoost reaches ROC-AUC 0.919 and PR-AUC 0.457. At the standard 0.5 threshold, accuracy is 0.963 — against a majority baseline of 0.961, so accuracy is meaningless here — with precision 0.694 and recall 0.080: only 59 of the 735 non-metals are found. The model ranks well and screens badly. The threshold analysis (Table 3) shows the remedy and its price: lowering the threshold to 0.2 raises recall to 0.616 and gives the best F1 of 0.528, at a precision of 0.462; at 0.1, recall is 0.789 but precision falls to 0.337. Any screening use of this classifier must operate near a threshold of 0.2, not 0.5. At that threshold the price is concrete: of every 100 crystals the classifier flags, about 54 are actually metals, and 38% of true non-metals are still missed — screening with this classifier is honest but expensive, and it is not a substitute for a better model. Second, regression on the 735 non-zero gaps only, again under grouped splits: R² 0.558, RMSE 0.996, MAE 0.763 eV. That is better than the first version's random-split value (R² 0.505), so grouped validation did not hurt here — but the sample is small (n = 735) and the result is provisional. Figure 5 (`fig5_bandgap_conformal.png`, panel a) shows the precision–recall trade-off.

### 4.4 Study D — Conformal uncertainty under shift

Split-conformal intervals at a nominal 90% level were tested on random rows and on nine held-out B metals, with the held-out metal absent from both training and calibration: Stage 6 tested tellurium, tungsten, and magnesium, and Stage 6b added six more — rhenium, tantalum, cobalt, manganese, zinc, and gallium — chosen to span the full range of Stage 4 difficulty. On random test rows the promise holds: coverage 0.899, mean width 0.370 eV/atom, point R² 0.423. Under family shift the picture splits by metal. Tellurium is covered at 0.923 with mean width 0.374 eV/atom — but its point R² is −0.142, so the intervals cover only because they are wide (±0.19 eV/atom) while the point predictions are worse than the mean. Coverage is not accuracy. Magnesium is covered at 0.945 (width 0.381 eV/atom, point R² 0.423): both the intervals and the point predictions are good. The six new metals extend the pattern in both directions: zinc (0.984, width 0.374 eV/atom, point R² 0.347) and gallium (0.975, width 0.380 eV/atom, point R² 0.363) hold the promise, cobalt (0.953, width 0.374 eV/atom, point R² −0.559) and manganese (0.890, width 0.372 eV/atom, point R² −0.721) sit near it, while tantalum (0.745, width 0.369 eV/atom, point R² −3.004) and rhenium (0.632, width 0.360 eV/atom, point R² −4.713) fail it. Across all nine metals coverage runs from 0.632 to 0.984 while the mean width stays within 0.360–0.381 eV/atom: the intervals do not widen where the model is worse, so on the hard metals the model is confidently wrong. The three failures — rhenium, tungsten (0.690, width 0.374 eV/atom, point R² −4.211), and tantalum — are exactly the three metals with the worst point predictions, so coverage under shift fails where accuracy fails. Under domain shift the conformal guarantee does not apply, and this dataset shows the failure concretely rather than hypothetically. Figure 5 (`fig5_bandgap_conformal.png`, panel b) shows coverage against the nominal 0.90 line.

### 4.5 Site-aware features recover transfer (Stages 3b and 3c)

The site-aware feature set (232 columns) was tested on leave-one-B-out only — the hard test, not random cross-validation. Pooled over all held-out predictions, tuned XGBoost reaches R² 0.733 (RMSE 0.077, MAE 0.060 eV/atom), against 0.086 for Magpie-only; per-family mean R² is 0.387, median 0.497, with 46 of 52 families positive, best Ni 0.747, worst Cr −1.517. Ridge regression with the same features is worse than Magpie-only ridge (pooled R² −0.193 against 0.206), so the gain is specific to the tree model: a linear model extrapolates the new physics features off a cliff, while a tree assigns an unseen metal to the nearest leaf it has seen. The 0.733 result was held to the leak diagnostics described in Methods and survived all four; it is reported as a real result.

The ablation (Stage 3c) then asks how much of that recovery actually needs B-site physics. Removing all B information — one-hot, physics, and derived columns — still gives pooled R² 0.683 (RMSE 0.084, MAE 0.064 eV/atom; per-family mean 0.335, median 0.431, minimum Ta −0.632, maximum Ni 0.747). The three-way comparison is therefore: Magpie-only 0.086, no-B-information 0.683, full site-aware 0.733. B-site physics adds only +0.050. The per-family contrasts show the addition is not uniform: chromium improves from −1.517 (site-aware) to +0.446 (no-B-information), while rhenium falls from 0.300 to −0.497, tungsten from 0.226 to −0.098, and tantalum from −0.062 to −0.632. The mechanism that fits all of this is that most of the gain comes from giving A and X explicitly — site resolution — plus the Magpie residual, which then identifies the B contribution by subtraction. B-site physics helps the hard heavy metals (W, Re, Ta) and hurts chromium. Any paper that claimed "B-site physics is the key" would be overstating the evidence; the honest claim is that site resolution is the key, and B-site physics is a modest, metal-dependent addition. To be explicit about what was tested: this ablation covers all 52 B metals (52 rows in `results_siteaware_nob_v2.csv`), and it is a different check from the leakage diagnostics in Methods, which test the 0.733 result for leakage and are not an ablation. Section 4.6 tests the same mechanism from the other side — what the fitted model actually relies on — and the two views must be read together.

### 4.6 What the model relies on: SHAP (Stages 9a and 9a2)

SHAP assigns every feature a fair share of the credit for each prediction; mean |SHAP| is how much a feature moves predictions on average, in eV/atom. Two different questions must be kept apart. SHAP measures reliance — what the fitted model leans on. Ablation measures necessity — what happens to accuracy when a group of features is removed. They can disagree, and on this data they do, in an instructive way.

On familiar crystals (Stage 9a, random 80/20 split, seed 0), the site-aware model reaches test R² 0.812 (RMSE 0.064, MAE 0.050 eV/atom) against 0.440 for Magpie-only (RMSE 0.111, MAE 0.086) — interpolation numbers, not transfer numbers. Group shares for the site-aware model are Magpie 39.8%, B-physics 31.9%, derived 16.1%, A-physics 11.6%, B one-hot 0.5%, X one-hot 0.1%, X-physics 0.0% (Table 6). Per feature, however, B-physics is the strongest group (mean |SHAP| 0.00559 per feature, against 0.000634 for a Magpie feature), and the derived group is stronger still (0.00678); Magpie wins the group total only because it has 132 features. ### 4.7 The graph-model comparison: pretrained MEGNet zero-shot (Stage 9b)

The comparison a reviewer would demand is a graph model on the same split — and it has now been run. The pretrained MEGNet formation-energy model (Eform_MP_2019), trained on 133,420 Materials Project structures, was evaluated zero-shot on all 52 leave-one-B-out families (18,928 predictions, 3.0 minutes total). Two warnings come first, because the raw numbers will otherwise be misread. First, MEGNet's energies are on the Materials Project scale and ours are on the Castelli scale (oxygen referenced to water vapour, not O₂): for SrTiO₃, MEGNet predicts −3.434 eV/atom against the Castelli value of −0.048, an offset of −3.386 eV/atom. The pooled raw R² is therefore −77.753 (RMSE 1.318, MAE 1.103 eV/atom, bias −0.874) — a units difference, not a demonstration that the model is useless. The honest numbers are the scale-free ranking and the recalibrated score. Second, about 6.2% of Castelli rows share a reduced formula with the Materials Project (concentrated in the oxides, 39.8% of O₃ rows), so the zero-shot numbers are an upper bound for a clean pretrained model; the catastrophic raw R² is itself evidence against memorisation. That 6.2% was measured against the October 2018 Materials Project snapshot (83,989 rows), while MEGNet was trained on the April 2019 snapshot (133,420 structures), so 6.2% is a lower bound on the true overlap — the upper-bound framing is conservative.

After a two-parameter affine recalibration fitted per family on non-held-out rows only (*a* ≈ 0.100, *b* ≈ +0.351, stable across all families), pooled leave-one-B-out R² is 0.561 (RMSE 0.098, MAE 0.077 eV/atom), with pooled Spearman rank correlation 0.752. Per family, the calibrated R² is positive for 30 of 52 metals (best Tl 0.762, Mg 0.745, Zn 0.687, Al 0.677, Cd 0.669, In 0.668, Hg 0.657, Pb 0.652) and negative for 22 (worst W −1.866, Re −1.427, Mo −1.261, Rb −1.168, Cs −1.014, Cr −0.696, K −0.653). Spearman is positive for every family (0.39–0.90), so MEGNet ranks within every family better than chance even where its calibrated R² is negative. The four-way pooled comparison is therefore: Magpie-only XGBoost 0.086, calibrated MEGNet 0.561, no-B-information XGBoost 0.683, site-aware XGBoost 0.733 (Table 9). The graph model beats composition-only by a wide margin and ranks well, but it does not beat the site-aware model — and it does not even beat the no-B-information ablation. Structure alone, as embodied in a pretrained graph network, does not close the gap that site resolution closes. That is a statement about this pretrained model on this grid, not about graph models in general: MEGNet was trained on relaxed Materials Project structures, while ours are ideal cubic, and no graph model was trained from scratch here — that remains future work.

## 5. Discussion

### 5.1 Why composition-only models fail on new elements

The failure has a precise mechanism, and it is visible in the data before any model is fitted. Magpie features are statistics over the composition: means, ranges, and deviations of elemental properties, with no record of which element sits on which site. Two crystals that swap A and B therefore receive identical features and different labels, which is exactly the 0.543 ceiling of Study A. Leave-one-B-out makes the same blindness fatal rather than merely limiting: the held-out metal's contribution to the composition statistics is the only signal that distinguishes its crystals from the rest, and a composition aggregate cannot carry it. The site-aware experiment confirms the diagnosis by removing it: once A and X are given explicitly, the same algorithm, with the same tuning, goes from 0.086 to 0.683 without ever being told anything about the held-out B metal. The residual +0.050 from B-site physics is real but secondary. In short, the model was not failing because the chemistry is unpredictable; it was failing because the representation erased the chemistry's structure.

The ridge-versus-XGBoost split in both Study B and Stage 3b has a second, model-class explanation that should not be confused with the representation story. Ridge regression extrapolates linearly: for an unseen anion or an unseen metal with extreme physics values, its prediction follows the fitted line out of the training range, which is why X-out ridge reaches −9.958 and site-aware ridge reaches −0.193. A tree cannot extrapolate at all — it returns the value of the nearest leaf — which bounds the damage and is why XGBoost survives X-out at 0.375 and benefits from site-aware features at 0.733. "Trees do not extrapolate" is usually listed as a weakness; on this task it is the reason they fail less badly.

One more distinction belongs here, because §4.6 is easy to misread. SHAP tells us what the fitted model leans on; the ablation tells us what happens to accuracy when a feature group is removed. On this data they point in different directions on average — Magpie takes 42% of the SHAP credit under shift, yet removing all B information leaves the accuracy gain almost intact — because within a held-out family the B metal is constant and all within-family variation comes from A and X, which Magpie encodes in aggregate form. The correct reading is that site resolution makes Magpie's own signal usable for transfer; tungsten, where B-physics both leads SHAP and is necessary, is the metal-dependent exception.

### 5.2 Literature comparison

Perovskite machine learning is an established field, and this work does not introduce it. Chen *et al.* [6] trained XGBoost on 3,720 ABX$_3$ and 2,660 double perovskites with nearly 300 Matminer descriptors and reported R² 0.873 (RMSE 0.5868 eV) for band gap, with SHAP analysis showing formation energy among the most influential descriptors. That number is not comparable to ours, for four reasons that the comparison makes explicit rather than hidden: their target is the band gap, whose spread is wide and therefore easy to explain in R² terms, while our formation-energy target has a standard deviation of only 0.149 eV/atom; their data are curated and mostly unique compositions, so there is no A/B-twin ceiling; they use roughly 300 descriptors against our 132; and they report a random split, with no leave-one-element-out test. Notably, their SHAP analysis identifies formation energy — itself a DFT-computed target — as an important feature for predicting the gap; our design forbids that class of feature outright. Deng *et al.* [7] report R² 0.928 (RMSE 0.301 eV/atom) for ABX$_3$ formation energy in the abstract (0.939 after feature reduction, as stated in the paper's conclusions), again on a curated set under a random split. The lesson of both papers is not that our models are worse; it is that random-split R² on curated perovskite data and family-out R² on a factorial grid are different examinations, and the literature's headline numbers are almost all from the easier one.

The composition ceiling itself has independent support. Asaria *et al.* [14] quantified the same degeneracy on this data as a floor of 0.379 eV per 5-atom formula unit (0.076 eV/atom) — the source mislabels the unit as eV/atom, but the numbers are consistent only per formula unit — and our ceiling implies a residual MAE of ≈0.08 eV/atom, in agreement. Asaria *et al.* also show the structure advantage persists under a SOAP-cluster leave-out split on the same data, and declines with distance from the training data. Martirossyan *et al.* [20] note A/B-swapped pairs explicitly as polymorphic pairs, though in a generative structure-prediction context and without computing a ceiling or floor. The degeneracy is therefore recognised; what this paper adds is the R²-ceiling framing (0.543), the pooled variance decomposition, the explicit A/B-twin proof on the full grid, and the link to a specific model's measured failure (best grouped model 0.475 below the ceiling; family-out Magpie collapsing to 0.086).

The closest prior use of site-resolved features on this exact grid is Cherukara and Mannodi-Kanakkithodi [13], who built a 75-feature site-resolved elemental fingerprint (25 elemental properties per site, pruned to 15 by correlation, × 5 sites) and trained a neural network, random forest, and kernel ridge regression on it. Their evaluation, however, was random-split interpolation on the 735 non-zero-gap compounds only (formation-energy R² 0.80–0.88, RMSE 0.13–0.20 eV), with no element hold-out, no ceiling, no ablation, and no conformal test; they suggest extending to "completely new element choices" but never test it. Their numbers must not be compared directly with ours: different subset (n = 735), a possibly different target definition, and units not stated in the excerpts read. The general principle that elemental physics helps unseen-element prediction has support elsewhere: Mahdavi *et al.* [16] show that enriching graph models with elemental descriptors restores near-in-distribution accuracy for unseen elements on general materials data (though on a different dataset, a different model class, and random element exclusion, not leave-one-B-out on perovskites). The tolerance-factor lineage behind our derived features goes back to Bartel *et al.* [19], whose new tolerance factor τ classifies perovskite vs non-perovskite at 92% accuracy on 576 ABX₃ compounds.

The leave-one-element-out protocol itself is established. Li *et al.* [15] ran over 700 out-of-distribution tasks, including leave-one-element-out, on Materials Project and JARVIS formation-energy data, and found high R² for most held-out elements but failure for H, F, and O — and that most heuristically-defined out-of-distribution tests are actually interpolation, because test data lie inside the training domain. Durdy *et al.* [18] provide the counter-expectation: under kernelised leave-one-cluster-out cross-validation, domain knowledge usually does *not* improve machine-learning performance (band gap the exception). Our site-aware result beats that expectation, which is why the ablation in §4.5 matters: it shows exactly where domain knowledge helps (site resolution) and where it adds little (B-site physics). Our application of the protocol to the Castelli grid — all 52 B metals, all 7 X anions, pooled reporting — is the new part, not the protocol.

Structure-aware models set the other pole of the comparison. Crystal graph convolutional networks [4] and materials graph networks (MEGNet) [5] read the crystal structure directly and dominate the MatBench leaderboard: on the MatBench perovskite task the best reported mean absolute errors are 0.0269 eV per unit cell (coGN), 0.0313 (MatterVial MODNet), and 0.0673 (MODNet), against 0.2005 for the Automatminer reference, as tabulated by Gouvêa *et al.* [10]. Our leave-one-B-out XGBoost MAE of 0.107 eV/atom — 0.535 eV per 5-atom cell — is far above those values, which is expected: the leaderboard numbers come from random splits with structure-aware models, while ours come from a family-out split with a composition-based model. The direct perovskite comparison of graph and traditional models by Liu *et al.* [8] is the closest published analogue to our design: on all-inorganic perovskites, CGCNN beat the best gradient-boosting model by more than 20% in RMSE for single perovskites (formation-energy RMSE 0.205 eV/atom, band-gap RMSE 0.718 eV, energy-above-hull RMSE 0.167 eV/atom). We have now run this comparison ourselves (§4.7): pretrained MEGNet zero-shot reaches pooled R² 0.561 after recalibration on the same leave-one-B-out split — better than Magpie-only (0.086) by a wide margin, but below both the no-B-information ablation (0.683) and the site-aware model (0.733). No prior graph-model leave-one-B-out on this grid was found in the literature check, so the comparison itself is a new result — but it must be reported as zero-shot pretrained MEGNet, not as a trained graph model. A caution from the other direction: Chenebuah *et al.* [21] report formation-energy R² of 99% with support-vector regression on 1,453 perovskites — on an explicitly deduplicated set, and using energy above hull as a feature, a DFT-derived quantity our design forbids; the comparison shows how much reported accuracy depends on what is allowed as input.

Two further points of contact with the literature are worth stating. First, the zero-inflated band gap is not peculiar to this dataset: Suhendar [9], working with 45,570 perovskites (not the Castelli grid), reports a distribution "dominated by metallic or nearly metallic compounds with zero band gaps" and a best testing R² of 0.791 for band-gap regression with LightGBM — a number that, like our all-rows R² of 0.288, is flattered by the zero mass, and comes from an all-rows random split. Our classification-first treatment is the correct response to that structure, and the literature's plain-regression numbers should be read with the same suspicion. Second, the conformal result has a general antecedent: Boruah [17] shows that split-conformal conditional-coverage failure survives null correction on four of five materials datasets under random splits. What Boruah establishes is that conformal degradation under shift is a general materials-ML phenomenon; our contribution is the element-shift version, with a metal-dependent failure pattern (rhenium 0.632, tungsten 0.690, and tantalum 0.745 failing; tellurium 0.923, magnesium 0.945, cobalt 0.953, zinc 0.984, and gallium 0.975 holding). Finally, the MatBench benchmark itself [3] is the reason this dataset exists in a standardized form; our contribution to it is a negative result about one of its tasks under a split the leaderboard does not use.

### 5.3 What is new, and what is not

What is not new: machine learning for perovskites; gradient boosting on perovskite properties; Magpie features [2]; tolerance-factor reasoning [19]; conformal prediction [11]; the Castelli dataset [1]; the leave-one-element-out protocol [15]; site-resolved elemental features on this grid [13]; the composition-degeneracy floor [14]; and the observation that graph models beat composition models [4,5,8,10]. None of these is claimed here.

To our knowledge, no prior study has combined these five elements on the Castelli grid: a pooled composition-only ceiling derived from A/B-swapped twins; pooled leave-one-B-element-out reporting; a site-resolved feature test under that extrapolation split; an ablation removing all B-site information; and split-conformal coverage measured under element shift. The individual ingredients are not new, and we cite them. The composition-degeneracy floor on this data was quantified by Asaria *et al.* [14]; site-resolved elemental features on this grid were used by Cherukara and Mannodi-Kanakkithodi [13] under random splits; and the leave-one-element-out protocol was established for general materials data by Li *et al.* [15]. What is new is the application of those tools to the extrapolation question on this grid, and the two findings that come out of it: site resolution, not B-site physics, carries the transfer gain (pooled R² 0.683 without any B information, against 0.733 with it), and conformal coverage under element shift is metal-dependent, failing for rhenium (0.632), tungsten (0.690), and tantalum (0.745) while holding for tellurium (0.923), magnesium (0.945), cobalt (0.953), zinc (0.984), and gallium (0.975).

In summary, while the leave-one-element-out protocol and composition-degeneracy ceiling build on established foundations, both the no-B-information site-resolution ablation and the metal-dependent conformal breakdown under element shift represent novel empirical findings on this grid. This draft does not claim "first" for the partial claims: each has a named prior. What is claimed is the specific combination and the specific numbers: the 0.543 ceiling in R² form tied to the model results; the pooled 0.086 collapse and 0.733 recovery on the full grid; the 0.683 ablation localising the gain to site resolution; the nine-metal conformal test; and the zero-shot pretrained MEGNet comparison (0.561), for which no prior graph-model leave-one-B-out on this grid was found. The verdicts here mean "no prior work found", not "no prior work exists": the search covered English, web-indexed sources to November 2026, and a final pre-submission pass (Google Scholar and Semantic Scholar) is still recommended. The one open lead from the literature check has now been verified (2026-11-25): Tan *et al.* [22] benchmark graph networks for out-of-distribution materials prediction with uncertainty quantification on this same Castelli grid, but their uncertainty method is Monte Carlo dropout plus deep evidential regression, not split conformal, and their splits are OFM/SOAP clusters, not leave-one-element-out — so the conformal-under-element-shift verdict stands as new, and Tan is cited here as the closest OOD+UQ prior on this grid.

### 5.4 Limitations

This work has limits that bound every claim above. The dataset is a synthetic factorial grid, not a natural distribution of perovskites: every combination of 52 A, 52 B, and 7 X appears exactly once per A–X pairing, which is not how real materials are distributed, and performance on this grid need not transfer to experimentally realized compounds. All band gaps are DFT (GLLBSC) gaps, which are systematically biased against experiment; the band-gap results are results about a DFT target. There is no energy-above-hull data, so nothing here speaks to stability, and the 74 negative formation energies must not be read as 74 stable materials. Band-gap regression rests on 735 rows and is provisional. The tuned model configurations were fixed from a diagnostic fold in the first version of this work, so the reported scores carry mild optimism. Only three seeds were used, which is limited statistical evidence; means are reported without tight uncertainty claims, and no second dataset was tested — the results are evidence on one grid, not a general law. A graph model was run, but only as a pretrained zero-shot model (§4.7); no graph model was trained from scratch on this grid, so the comparison with structure-aware methods is still incomplete, and the zero-shot numbers are an upper bound because of the ~6.2% formula overlap with the Materials Project (measured against the October 2018 snapshot; the model trained on the April 2019 snapshot, so 6.2% is a lower bound on the true overlap). Conformal coverage under shift is an empirical observation on nine metals, not a guarantee, and the intervals are marginal over the calibration set. No candidate perovskites were screened and no fresh DFT validation was performed, so the paper offers no usable material prediction. Finally, chromium remains poorly predicted even by the site-aware model (per-family R² −1.517), and the site-aware ridge model fails outright (−0.193): site resolution helps one model class on this task, not all of them.

## 6. Conclusion

Composition-only machine learning interpolates perovskites and does not extrapolate to new B-site metals. On the complete Castelli grid, Magpie-only models reach R² 0.43–0.48 on familiar crystals — below a 0.543 ceiling imposed by A/B-swapped twins — and fall to pooled R² 0.086 on a new B metal, with ridge regression failing catastrophically on a new anion at −9.958. Band-gap screening must be treated as classification first, because 96.1% of gaps are zero, and conformal intervals keep their 90% promise on random crystals (coverage 0.899) but range from 0.632 (rhenium) to 0.984 (zinc) across nine held-out metals under shift. Site-aware features recover leave-one-B-out prediction to pooled R² 0.733 for XGBoost, and an ablation shows that most of that recovery comes from resolving the A and X sites explicitly (0.683 without any B information), with B-site physics adding only +0.050. SHAP shows the model leans on Magpie statistics plus B-site physics on familiar crystals, and still leans on Magpie for unseen metals on average — except tungsten, where B-site physics leads and is necessary — a reminder that feature reliance and feature necessity are different questions. A pretrained graph network reaches only 0.561 after recalibration on the same split: structure alone does not close the gap that site resolution closes. The practical conclusion is that the representation, not the algorithm, is the binding constraint on transfer in this space — and the next steps are training a graph model from scratch on this grid, testing robustness on more seeds and a second dataset, and screening candidates for fresh DFT validation.
